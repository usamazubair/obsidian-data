### Where it runs into trouble

**Joins across entities.** In Mongo you have two choices: embed the related data (duplicate it inside the parent document) or reference it and do the join in application code, or with `$lookup`. Embedding works until the related data changes independently or is accessed on its own — then you have the same fact copied in a hundred documents, and updating it means updating a hundred documents. Referencing avoids that, but now you're doing manual joins in your app or paying for `$lookup`, which doesn't optimize the way a relational planner does with decades of join-algorithm research behind it (hash joins, merge joins, index-aware nested loops). If your data has many-to-many relationships that get queried from multiple angles — orders to customers to products to inventory to shipments — a document model fights you the whole way.

**Multi-document transactions.** Mongo added ACID transactions across documents in version 4.0, but the document model _encourages_ you to design around ever needing one, by embedding things into a single document precisely so a single write stays atomic. When your actual business logic can't be squeezed into "one document, one write" — like moving money between two accounts, or decrementing inventory in one collection while creating an order in another — you're now using a feature that goes against the model's natural shape, and it costs more in performance than the equivalent single-row-locking transaction does in Postgres.

**Data integrity enforced by the database itself, not just by your app.** Postgres can guarantee, at the database level, that an `order.customer_id` always points to a real customer, that a `quantity` column is never negative, that two rows can't both claim the same unique `email`. In Mongo, without a schema (or with only soft validation), that correctness lives in your application code. That's fine as long as every service that ever writes to that collection is disciplined and bug-free, forever. It rarely stays that way once a codebase has multiple services, multiple developers, and a few years of history — you end up with orphaned references and inconsistent documents that nobody notices until a report comes out wrong.
